The Deep End

Earth + ocean science, for teenagers

Jump in the deep end. deep end.

The ocean covers about 71% of our planet. It makes about half of Earth's oxygen, shapes your weather, and soaks up heat that would otherwise bake us. Most of it has never been explored, and most of us barely learn about it in school. So let's go look.

Zone 1 Sunlight zone 0–200 m

The ocean keeps you alive. Every single day.

Sunlight reaches down about 200 meters. That's enough light for trillions of microscopic drifting organisms called phytoplankton to photosynthesize, just like plants on land. They're the base of almost every ocean food web, and they're doing something for you right now.

Try it

Breathe in. Then breathe again.

Scientists estimate that about half of Earth's oxygen is made in the ocean, mostly by phytoplankton. One microbe, Prochlorococcus, is so small that millions fit in a drop of seawater, and it may make up to 20% of the oxygen in our biosphere. The honest footnote: ocean life also uses up most of the oxygen it makes. The air in your lungs comes from a giant shared supply that land and sea have built up together over millions of years, and the ocean is half the team keeping it full.

What else the ocean does for you

  • ~90% of the extra heat

    Air conditioner

    The ocean has absorbed about 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases. Without it, the air would be far hotter. But warmer water also fuels stronger hurricanes and bleaches coral reefs.

  • ~30% of our CO₂

    Carbon sponge

    It absorbs about 30% of the carbon dioxide people release. That slows climate change, but it also makes seawater more acidic, which makes it harder for oysters, corals, and some plankton to build their shells.

  • 3.1 billion people

    Dinner table

    Fish and other seafood, from oceans, rivers, and farms, give about 3.1 billion people at least a fifth of the animal protein they eat. They also support more than 600 million jobs and livelihoods.

  • Drugs from the deep

    Medicine cabinet

    A cancer drug called trabectedin was first found in a sea squirt. A powerful painkiller, ziconotide, is a lab-made copy of a compound in cone snail venom. Nobody knows what else is still out there.

  • Moves heat worldwide

    Weather machine

    Ocean currents carry heat around the planet like a giant conveyor belt. That shapes rain, droughts, heat waves, and storms everywhere, including New York.

  • ~97% of Earth's water

    Water tank

    Almost all of Earth's water is in the ocean. Much of the rain that falls on land started as evaporated seawater, including rain that fills the reservoirs New York City drinks from.

Zone 2 Twilight zone 200–1,000 m

Earth science isn't far away. It's in New York.

Down here, sunlight fades to a dim blue. Every night, huge numbers of fish, shrimp, and jellies swim up toward the surface to feed, then sink back down before sunrise. It's the biggest migration on Earth, and it happens every day without most people noticing.

Earth science is a lot like that. Here's where it has shown up in New York.

  1. ~20,000 years agoLong Island

    Long Island is glacier leftovers

    During the last ice age, a giant ice sheet bulldozed rock and sand south from New England, then melted. The ridges it left behind, called moraines, form the hilly spine of Long Island.

  2. Last ice ageCentral Park

    Central Park has glacier scratches

    Look closely at big rock outcrops like Umpire Rock, near 63rd Street. The parallel grooves were carved by stones dragged along under the ice. Those huge boulders sitting around for no reason? Glaciers dropped them. Geologists call them erratics.

  3. Twice a dayHudson River

    The Hudson flows both ways

    The lower Hudson is an estuary, where salty ocean water meets fresh river water. Ocean tides push upriver all the way to Troy, about 153 miles north. The Lenape name for the river, Muhheacannituck, is often translated as "the river that flows two ways."

  4. Since 1900The Battery, Manhattan

    The water is rising

    Sea level at the Battery has risen a little more than a foot since 1900, partly because the land there is slowly sinking. Every storm surge now starts from a higher starting line.

  5. October 2012NYC + the coast

    Hurricane Sandy

    A storm tide of about 14 feet at the Battery flooded subway tunnels, knocked out power across much of lower Manhattan, and killed 44 people in the city. It showed how much the ocean shapes city life.

  6. June 2023The whole city

    The sky turned orange

    Smoke from wildfires in Canada drifted hundreds of miles south and pushed the city's air quality index above 400, into the "hazardous" range. It was the worst air in New York's modern records. Atmospheric scientists tracked where the smoke would go next.

  7. April 2024New Jersey + NYC

    The ground shook

    A magnitude 4.8 earthquake centered in Tewksbury, New Jersey, about 40 miles west of the city, shook the whole region. More people reported feeling it to the USGS than any earthquake on record. The East Coast has old faults too.

  8. Right now~100 miles offshore

    A canyon under the sea

    The Hudson Canyon is the largest underwater canyon off the US Atlantic coast, up to 7.5 miles wide and 2 miles deep. NOAA has proposed protecting it as a national marine sanctuary, but as of 2026 that decision is still pending. Public support matters for choices like this.

Zone 3 Midnight zone 1,000–4,000 m

No sunlight from here down. Grab a flashlight.

Below 1,000 meters it's pitch black and close to freezing, and the pressure is more than 100 times what you feel at the surface. The only light down here is the light animals make themselves. In one long study off California, 76% of the animals scientists filmed could glow. Move your cursor, or tap, to search the dark.

0 of 6 found

Those specks drifting down are real. Scientists call them marine snow, and they're food for the deep. Also, notice how many of these animals are red? Red light doesn't reach this deep, so red animals look black and stay hidden.

Zone 4 The abyss 4,000–6,000 m

We have better maps of Mars than of this.

The abyss is cold, dark, and enormous. Spacecraft have mapped the whole surface of Mars, but most of our own seafloor is still a blurry outline. That means the biggest unexplored places on Earth are right here, waiting for your generation.

Myth or fact?

Pick an answer on each card

  • The deep sea is basically empty.

    It's a myth.

    It's full of life. Hydrothermal vents host giant tube worms, crabs, and shrimp, and scientists find new species on almost every deep-sea expedition.

  • Most of the deep ocean has never been seen by humans.

    It's a fact.

    According to NOAA, explorers have seen less than 0.001% of the deep seafloor, even counting cameras on robots.

  • Earth science is just about rocks.

    It's a myth.

    It covers oceans, weather, climate, earthquakes, volcanoes, fresh water, and even space weather. A lot of it is coding and data work.

  • You have to live by the beach to become an ocean scientist.

    It's a myth.

    Ocean scientists work at universities all over the country, study satellite data from anywhere, and steer underwater robots over the internet. And New York has a coast anyway.

  • Mount Everest would fit inside the Mariana Trench.

    It's a fact.

    The Challenger Deep is about 10,935 meters deep. Everest is 8,849 meters tall. Its peak would still be about 2 km underwater.

  • Some deep-sea animals live for centuries.

    It's a fact.

    Scientists estimate some Greenland sharks live about 400 years. Some deep-sea corals have been growing for more than 4,000 years.

Zone 5 Hadal zone 6,000–10,935 m

The deepest jobs on Earth are hiring.

The hadal zone is made of deep-sea trenches, named after Hades, the Greek underworld. Only a few dozen people have ever reached the bottom of the Challenger Deep. Hundreds have been to space.

The people who explore it next are in school right now. Maybe that's you.

The bottom Challenger Deep 10,935 m

You touched the bottom. Here's how to keep going.

You don't need a lab, a boat, or money to get into earth and ocean science. Pick one thing from this list and do it this week.

5 minutes

From your phone

  • Nautilus Live

    Watch live deep-sea robot dives and send questions to the scientists on board.

  • NOAA Ocean Exploration

    Livestreams, photos, and videos from NOAA's deep-sea expeditions.

  • GLOBE Observer

    A free NASA app (ages 13+). Photograph clouds or trees, and your data goes to real scientists.

  • iNaturalist

    Snap a photo of any living thing (ages 13+). The community helps identify it, and researchers use the data.

  • Plankton Portal on Zooniverse

    Help scientists identify plankton in real underwater images. If it wraps up, Zooniverse has other ocean projects.

A weekend

Around New York

  • Billion Oyster Project

    Help restore oyster reefs in New York Harbor. They've restored more than 183 million oysters so far, aiming for a billion by 2030.

  • American Museum of Natural History

    Stand under the 94-foot blue whale in the Hall of Ocean Life, then see rocks from deep inside the Earth in the Hall of Planet Earth.

  • New York Aquarium

    Its Ocean Wonders: Sharks! exhibit shows the sharks, rays, and sea turtles that live right off the Coney Island boardwalk.

  • Central Park glacier hunt

    Find the glacier scratches on Umpire Rock, on the west side near 63rd Street, behind Heckscher Playground.

A semester or summer

Go deeper

  • National Ocean Sciences Bowl

    A team competition for high schoolers covering ocean biology, chemistry, geology, and policy. The NY-area regional is paused for 2027, but teams can enter the virtual Sponge Bowl.

  • Science Olympiad

    Join or start a team. Earth science events cover topics like rocks and minerals, weather, and plate tectonics.

  • US Earth Science Olympiad

    Compete for a spot on the team that represents the US at the International Earth Science Olympiad.

  • Lamont-Doherty summer research

    Columbia's earth science lab runs a six-week summer research program, mostly at Piermont Marsh on the Hudson, for students 16 and up. Applications have closed in early spring.

  • New York Harbor School

    A public high school on Governors Island with programs in professional diving, vessel operations, aquaculture, marine biology research, and ocean engineering.
